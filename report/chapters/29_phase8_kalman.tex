\subsection{Goal}

Replace the fixed-window refit with genuine adaptive estimation and ask whether it
improves the \emph{traded, net-of-cost} result --- not merely the fit. Phase~\ref{ch:phase6}
had already shown that the relationship drifts (the static hedge fails the ADF test while
the rolling one passes), so the question was not whether adaptation is needed but which
kind pays.

\subsection{What was built}

\code{scripts/model/kalman.py} implements the local-level state-space model
\eqref{eq:kalman:state}--\eqref{eq:kalman:obs} with state $=[\alpha_t,\beta_{1,t},\dots,
\beta_{k,t}]^{\top}$ (here $d=4$ for the three-name basket, $d=2$ for the two-name
baskets) and design row $z_t=[1,x_t^{\top}]$. The recursion is the standard
predict/update of Chapter~\ref{ch:kalman}:
\begin{align*}
  \text{predict:}\quad & \beta_{t\mid t-1}=\beta_{t-1},\quad P_{t\mid t-1}=P_{t-1}+Q,\\
  \text{innovate:}\quad & v_t = y_t - z_t^{\top}\beta_{t\mid t-1},\quad
  S_t = z_t^{\top}P_{t\mid t-1}z_t + R,\\
  \text{update:}\quad & K_t = P_{t\mid t-1}z_t/S_t,\quad
  \beta_t = \beta_{t\mid t-1} + K_t v_t,\quad
  P_t = (I-K_tz_t^{\top})P_{t\mid t-1}(I-K_tz_t^{\top})^{\top} + K_tRK_t^{\top}.
\end{align*}
The covariance update is in \emph{Joseph form} \eqref{eq:joseph} for the numerical reason
given in Section~\ref{sec:kalman:joseph}.

Three design choices worth recording:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The signal is the standardised innovation} $z^{\text{spread}}_t=v_t/\sqrt{S_t}$
    \eqref{eq:kalman:signal}, which is $N(0,1)$ under the model, so the $2.0/0.5$ bands
    carry a known probabilistic meaning rather than an empirical one.
  \item \textbf{No-lookahead by construction}: the state that scores bar $t$ is the
    \emph{predictive} $\beta_{t\mid t-1}$, which depends only on bars $\le t-1$. Bar $t$
    is fed into the update only after the signal has been recorded. This is the same
    timing as the rolling window ending at $t-1$, so the two hedges are directly
    comparable in one pipeline.
  \item \textbf{One hyper-parameter.} $R$ is the warm-up (120-bar) OLS residual variance
    and $P_0=R(A_0^{\top}A_0)^{-1}$ is the warm-up OLS posterior covariance; the state
    noise is $Q_{ii}=q^2R$ for a single dimensionless dial $q$, swept over
    $\{10^{-5},10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2},10^{-1}\}$. One dial means one hypothesis to count
    in Chapter~\ref{ch:multipletesting}, which is a genuine methodological advantage over
    a multi-parameter adaptive scheme.
\end{itemize}
The module's output API is deliberately identical to
\code{baseline\_models.walk\_forward} (\code{\{z, beta, intercept\}}), so the Kalman hedge
drops into the same \code{simulate} call and inherits the same cost model, execution
delay, and sizing. That is what makes the comparison an experiment.

\subsection{Synthetic validation first}

\code{scripts/test\_kalman.py} runs before any real-data claim, on data where the truth is
known:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tracking error.} With betas that drift smoothly, the filtered
    $\hat\beta_t$ tracks the truth with RMSE $\mathbf{0.73}$ versus $\mathbf{3.37}$ for a
    fixed full-sample OLS beta --- a $4.6\times$ improvement, which is the quantitative
    version of ``the relationship drifts, so a fixed hedge is wrong''.
  \item \textbf{No-lookahead.} Perturbing future bars leaves $\beta_{t\mid t-1}$
    bit-identical; the test fails if the recursion is ever reordered to update before
    scoring.
  \item \textbf{The filtered spread is the right kind of object.} ADF rejects a unit root
    in the filtered residual ($p<10^{-3}$) with an OU half-life of $\approx8$ bars --- so
    the Kalman spread is stationary and mean-reverting on the same scale as the rolling one
    ($9.4$ bars, Phase~\ref{ch:phase6}), and the two are comparable as trading objects.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Findings}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{K\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{A smooth, low-turnover hedge is the adaptive win --- and it wins on cost,
    not on signal.} The best net Sharpe of anything tested in this project on the
    \code{ADBE} basket is Kalman $q=10^{-5}$ at $+0.113$ full sample, above rolling Ridge
    ($+0.093$) and rolling OLS ($+0.050$ to $+0.076$). Its \emph{gross} Sharpe
    ($0.195$) is \emph{lower} than every windowed OLS variant. So the improvement comes
    entirely from the cost side: 61 round trips versus 198--362, i.e.\ a $3$--$6\times$
    reduction in turnover, which by \eqref{eq:costdrag} removes most of the drag that
    Phase~\ref{ch:phase4} measured at $87\%$ of gross return. Because the
    \code{ADBE}--basket relationship is fairly stable over sixteen years, the recursive
    filter adds value by \emph{not} churning the hedge every bar the way a refit window
    does.
  \item \textbf{Adaptation is not monotone: over-tracking destroys the spread.} Raising
    $q$ so the filter follows the levels faster --- which \emph{maximises the Gaussian fit
    likelihood on the training data} --- makes the residual whiter and removes the
    persistent deviation that is the tradable mispricing. Net Sharpe falls from $+0.113$ at
    $q=10^{-5}$ to $-0.042$ at $q=10^{-2}$ and $-0.114$ at $q=10^{-1}$ (where the filter
    tracks so closely that the threshold policy fires twice in sixteen years). This is the
    state-space echo of the Phase~\ref{ch:phase6} finding that the \emph{causal rolling}
    spread, not a fast-tracking fit, is the stationary tradeable object, and it is the
    cleanest illustration in the whole project of the bias--variance trade
    \eqref{eq:biasvariance} operating on the \emph{signal} rather than on the fit: a filter
    with zero bias against the data has no predictable residual left to trade.
  \item \textbf{Robustness on 2022+ is a real advantage, but the selection rule fails.}
    Low-$q$ Kalman stays net-positive out of sample on 2022+ ($+0.088$ to $+0.111$) while
    the default-window OLS/Ridge hedges are net-negative there ($-0.031$ to $-0.527$);
    only the very short rolling OLS $W{=}60$ does better ($+0.518$), and it pays for that
    with 362 round trips and a full-sample net Sharpe of only $+0.076$. But: choosing $q$
    on the 2019--2021 validation window by net Sharpe selects $q=10^{-3}$, which then
    performs \emph{badly} out of sample ($-0.257$). The smooth limit $q=10^{-5}$ was
    chosen by the reasoning of Section~\ref{sec:kalman:steady}, not by the validation rule.
    Reporting that the mechanical selection rule failed is more informative than reporting
    the best cell --- and it is a concrete instance of the general lesson that with a weak
    signal, hyper-parameter selection on a three-year window is mostly noise.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why the low-$q$ result is the theoretically expected one}

Section~\ref{sec:kalman:steady} derived that at steady state the filter is an EWMA with
decay $1-K^\ast$ and effective memory $1/K^\ast$ bars, where $K^\ast$ depends only on
$Q/R=q^2$. At $q=10^{-5}$, $K^\ast$ is tiny and the hedge is nearly constant with an
effective memory of thousands of bars --- longer than the sample. At $q=10^{-2}$, $K^\ast$
is order $10^{-1}$--$1$ and the memory is a few bars, shorter than the $9.4$-bar half-life
of the spread, so the filter absorbs the mispricing into the hedge instead of leaving it
in the residual. The crossover is therefore predictable in advance: \emph{the hedge should
adapt on a timescale longer than the reversion it is trying to measure}, or it will
measure nothing. The sweep confirms the prediction, and the prediction is what makes the
result a finding rather than a lucky cell.

\subsection{The C++ port, deferred and documented}

The Kalman recursion costs $\le250$ flops per bar at $d\le5$ (Section~\ref{sec:kalman:cost}),
which is well below the measured $\approx600$\,ns regression stage of the existing engine.
It is kept as a dependency-free Python reference in the research layer; porting it into
\code{src/model/} is straightforward (the Joseph-form update is $O(d^3)$ with no
allocation once $P$, $Q$, $K$ are pre-allocated) and is listed as future work in
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}. The deferral is an explicit allocation of effort: the C++ engine
exists to make large-scale \emph{simulation} fast and provably correct, and a per-bar
250-flop recursion in Python changes no conclusion in this book.

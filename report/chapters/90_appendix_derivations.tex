\section{Extended derivations}

Algebra that supports the main text but would interrupt it. Each subsection is

\subsection{The TSS $=$ ESS $+$ RSS decomposition}
\label{sec:der:anova}

With an intercept in $X$, $\hat\eps^{\top}\mathbf 1=0$ and $\hat\eps^{\top}\hat Y=0$.
Proof of the second: $\hat Y=HY$ and $\hat\eps=(I-H)Y$, so
$\hat Y^{\top}\hat\eps = Y^{\top}H(I-H)Y = Y^{\top}(H-H^2)Y = 0$ by idempotence
\eqref{eq:hat:idem}. Then, writing $\bar Y\mathbf 1 = H\bar Y\mathbf 1$ (the ones-column is
in $\mathcal C(X)$, so projecting a constant vector leaves it fixed),
\[
  \lVert Y-\bar Y\mathbf 1\rVert^2
  = \lVert (Y-\hat Y) + (\hat Y-\bar Y\mathbf 1)\rVert^2
  = \mathrm{RSS} + \lVert\hat Y-\bar Y\mathbf 1\rVert^2 + 2(Y-\hat Y)^{\top}(\hat Y-\bar Y\mathbf1),
\]
and the cross term is $\hat\eps^{\top}\hat Y - \bar Y\hat\eps^{\top}\mathbf 1 = 0-0=0$.
This is why $R^2$ of \eqref{eq:r2} is a genuine share of variation, and why it can never
decrease when a column is added to $X$ ($\mathcal C(X)$ grows, so $\mathrm{RSS}$ shrinks).

\subsection{Variance inflation factor}
\label{sec:der:vif}

Partition $X=[X_j\ \ X_{-j}]$ and use the block inverse: the $(j,j)$ element of
$(X^{\top}X)^{-1}$ equals $\bigl[X_j^{\top}(I-H_{-j})X_j\bigr]^{-1}$ with
$H_{-j}=X_{-j}(X_{-j}^{\top}X_{-j})^{-1}X_{-j}^{\top}$. Now
$X_j^{\top}(I-H_j)X_j = \mathrm{RSS}_j$, the residual sum of squares from regressing $X_j$
on the other columns, and $\mathrm{RSS}_j=\mathrm{TSS}_j(1-R_j^2)$ by
Section~\ref{sec:der:anova}. Hence
$\Var(\hat\beta_j)=\sigma^2/\bigl[\mathrm{TSS}_j(1-R_j^2)\bigr]$, which is \eqref{eq:vif}.

\subsection{Leave-one-out cross-validation in one fit}
\label{sec:der:loo}

For a linear smoother $\hat Y=SY$, removing observation $i$ gives
$S^{(-i)}$ and residuals $y_i-\hat y_i^{(-i)}$. Since removing a row is a rank-1 change to
$X^{\top}X$, Sherman--Morrison \eqref{eq:shermanmorrison} with
$u=v=x_i$, $A=X^{\top}X$ yields after one line of algebra
\begin{equation}
  y_i-\hat y_i^{(-i)} \;=\; \frac{y_i-\hat y_i}{1-S_{ii}},
  \label{eq:looidentity}
\end{equation}
which is the identity used in \eqref{eq:gcv}. Intuition: an observation with high leverage
$S_{ii}\to1$ determines its own fitted value, so removing it changes the fit a lot, and the
LOO residual must be inflated accordingly; as $S_{ii}\to1$ the LOO error diverges, which is
the formal statement that a leverage-1 point is unvalidated by the data.

\subsection{AR(1) moments and the OU correspondence}
\label{sec:der:ar1}

For $x_t-\mu=\phi(x_{t-1}-\mu)+\eta_t$ with $\lvert\phi\rvert<1$, iterating gives
$x_t-\mu=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi^j\eta_{t-j}$, so
$\Var=\sigma_\eta^2\sum_j\phi^{2j}=\sigma_\eta^2/(1-\phi^2)$ and
$\gamma(\ell)=\E[(x_t-\mu)(x_{t+\ell}-\mu)]=\sigma_\eta^2\sum_j\phi^j\phi^{j+\ell}
=\phi^{\ell}\sigma_\eta^2/(1-\phi^2)$, giving \eqref{eq:ar1}.

Matching to the OU stationary covariance \eqref{eq:ou:stationary} with sampling interval
$\Delta t$: $e^{-\theta\Delta t}=\phi$ and $\sigma^2/(2\theta)=\sigma_\eta^2/(1-\phi^2)$,
so
\begin{equation}
  \theta = -\frac{\ln\phi}{\Delta t},
  \qquad
  \sigma^2 = -\frac{2\ln\phi}{\Delta t}\cdot\frac{\sigma_\eta^2}{1-\phi^2},
  \qquad
  \tau_{1/2} = \frac{\ln2}{\theta} = \frac{\ln 2}{-\ln\phi}\,\Delta t .
  \label{eq:ar1ou}
\end{equation}
For $\phi$ close to 1, $-\ln\phi\approx1-\phi$, so $\theta\approx1-\phi$ and the Euler
regression \eqref{eq:ou:regression} (which estimates $b=\phi-1$) coincides with the exact
ML estimator. At our measured $\hat\theta=0.074$ ($\phi=0.929$) the approximation
$-\ln\phi=0.0737$ versus $1-\phi=0.0712$ differs by $3.5\%$ --- so the reported half-life
of $9.4$ bars carries a $\approx0.3$-bar systematic bias from the discretisation, which is
well inside its sampling error.

\subsection{Exact maximum-likelihood OU estimation}
\label{sec:der:oux}

The exact transition density from \eqref{eq:ou:moments} is
\[
  X_{t+1}\mid X_t \sim \mathcal N\Bigl(\mu+(X_t-\mu)e^{-\theta},\
  \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}\bigl(1-e^{-2\theta}\bigr)\Bigr),
\]
so with $a=e^{-\theta}$ the log-likelihood over $T-1$ transitions is that of an AR(1) with
coefficient $a$ and innovation variance $s^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}(1-a^2)$. Regressing
$X_{t+1}$ on $[1,X_t]$ gives $\hat a$, from which
$\hat\theta=-\ln\hat a$, $\hat\mu=\hat a_0/(1-\hat a)$ and
$\hat\sigma^2 = 2\hat\theta\,\hat s^2/(1-\hat a^2)$. This is the estimator to prefer when
$\hat\theta$ is large (fast reversion, e.g.\ intraday spreads), and it is the reason
\code{statkit.ou} reports both the regression coefficients and the implied
$(\theta,\mu,\sigma,\tau_{1/2})$ so the two conventions can be compared.

\subsection{Kalman steady-state gain}
\label{sec:der:kalman}

Scalar state, constant design $z_t\equiv1$: $P_{t\mid t-1}=P+Q$,
$K=(P+Q)/(P+Q+R)$, $P'=(1-K)(P+Q)$. Steady state $P'=P$:
\[
  P = (P+Q) - \frac{(P+Q)^2}{P+Q+R}
  \;\Longleftrightarrow\;
  \frac{(P+Q)^2}{P+Q+R} = Q
  \;\Longleftrightarrow\;
  P^2 + 2PQ + Q^2 = QP + QR + Q^2 ,
\]
so $P^2 + PQ - QR = 0$ and
\begin{equation}
  P = \frac{-Q + \sqrt{Q^2+4QR}}{2},
  \qquad
  K^\ast = \frac{P+Q}{P+Q+R} = \frac{Q+\sqrt{Q^2+4QR}}{2R+Q+\sqrt{Q^2+4QR}} .
  \label{eq:kalmansteadystate}
\end{equation}
Limiting behaviour, which is what Phase~\ref{ch:phase8} sweeps: as $q^2=Q/R\to0$,
$K^\ast\to\sqrt{Q/R}=q\to0$ (the hedge freezes, effective memory $1/K^\ast\to\infty$); as
$q\to\infty$, $K^\ast\to1$ (the hedge jumps to the new observation, memory $\to1$ bar). At
$q=10^{-5}$, $K^\ast\approx3\times10^{-3}$ and the effective memory is $\approx300$ bars;
at $q=10^{-2}$, $K^\ast\approx0.09$ and the memory is $\approx11$ bars --- shorter than the
$9.4$-bar half-life of the spread, which is the quantitative form of finding K2: the filter
absorbs the mispricing into the hedge before the trade can monetise it.

\subsection{$F$-test, and RESET as a special case}
\label{sec:der:ftest}

Under normality, if the restricted model imposes $k$ linear restrictions then
$\mathrm{RSS}_R-\mathrm{RSS}_U \sim \sigma^2\chi^2_k$ (noncentral if the restrictions are
false) and $\mathrm{RSS}_U\sim\sigma^2\chi^2_{T-n}$, independently, because the difference
and the unrestricted residual lie in orthogonal subspaces. Their ratio scaled by degrees of
freedom is $F_{k,T-n}$, giving \eqref{eq:ftest}.

RESET sets the restrictions $\gamma_2=\dots=\gamma_k=0$ on the coefficients of
$\hat y^2,\dots,\hat y^k$ in the augmented regression
(Section~\ref{sec:nonlinear:reset}). A useful equivalent form is the LM version
$T\cdot R^2_{\text{aux}}$ of the regression of $\hat\eps$ on $[X,\hat y^2,\dots,\hat y^k]$,
asymptotically $\chi^2_{k-1}$; \code{statkit.misspec.ramsey\_reset} computes both and the
unit test \code{test\_reset\_f\_form\_matches\_lm\_decision} asserts they agree in decision
on the same data --- a genuine consistency check between two asymptotically equivalent but
numerically different statistics.

\subsection{Variance of the Sharpe ratio}
\label{sec:der:sharpevar}

Write $\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}=\bar r/s$. The delta method on $g(\mu,\sigma^2)=\mu/\sigma$
with $\nabla g=(1/\sigma,\ -\mu/(2\sigma^3))$ and the asymptotic covariance of
$(\bar r, s^2)$, whose entries are $\sigma^2/T$, $0$ (for symmetric returns), and
$(\mu_4-\sigma^4)/T$ with $\mu_4=\E[(r-\mu)^4]$, gives
\[
  T\Var(\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}) \;\to\;
  \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\Bigl[\sigma^2 - 2\mu\cdot 0 + \frac{\mu^2}{4\sigma^4}(\mu_4-\sigma^4)\Bigr]
  = 1 + \tfrac12\mathrm{SR}^2\bigl(\kappa-1\bigr),
\]
with excess kurtosis $\kappa=\mu_4/\sigma^4-3$. Including the covariance term that appears
for skewed returns (through the third central moment $\mu_3$) yields \eqref{eq:sharpevar2}:
\[
  T\Var(\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}) \;\to\; 1 - \gamma_3\,\mathrm{SR} + \tfrac{\kappa+2}{4}\mathrm{SR}^2 ,
  \qquad \gamma_3 = \mu_3/\sigma^3 .
\]
Substituting the measured moments of the best strategy's daily net P\&L
($\gamma_3=+0.473$, $\kappa=+13.4$, $\mathrm{SR}_d=0.0218$, $T=3{,}979$):
\[
  \mathrm{se}(\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}_d) = \sqrt{\frac{1-0.473(0.0218)+\frac{15.4}{4}(0.0218)^2}{3979}}
  = \sqrt{\frac{0.9916}{3979}} = 0.0158 ,
\]
i.e.\ a $t$-statistic of $0.0218/0.0158=1.38$ --- the same conclusion as the HAC test in
Table~\ref{tab:power_dsr} ($t=1.57$; the small difference is the HAC correction for serial
correlation, which the delta-method variance does not include) and the same verdict. The
annualised standard error is $0.0158\sqrt{252}=0.250$ against an estimated annualised
Sharpe of $0.346$.

\subsection{Expected maximum of $M$ null statistics}
\label{sec:der:max}

For i.i.d.\ standard normals $Z_1,\dots,Z_M$, $P(\max_m Z_m\le z)=\Phi(z)^M$. The median of
the maximum solves $\Phi(z)^M=1/2$, i.e.\ $z=\Phi^{-1}\bigl((1/2)^{1/M}\bigr)$; for large
$M$ the mean is asymptotic to $\sqrt{2\ln M}$ with variance $\pi^2/(6\ln M)$ (the Gumbel
limit). Sketch: $1-\Phi(z)\sim\phi(z)/z$ as $z\to\infty$, so $\E[\max]\approx z$ where
$M\phi(z)/z\approx1$, i.e.\ $M e^{-z^2/2}/(z\sqrt{2\pi})\approx1$; taking logs,
$\ln M - z^2/2 - \ln(z\sqrt{2\pi})\approx0$, whose leading solution is
$z\approx\sqrt{2\ln M}$ (the $\ln z$ term gives the second-order correction
$-\frac{\ln\ln M + \ln 4\pi}{2\sqrt{2\ln M}}$).

At $M=44$: $\sqrt{2\ln 44}=2.75$; with the second-order correction, $2.75-0.31=2.44$.
At the measured effective $M_{\text{eff}}=10.7$: $\sqrt{2\ln 10.7}=2.18$. The observed best
HAC $t$ is $1.57$ --- below both. This is the cleanest single statement of the project's
negative result: \emph{the best of our 44 strategies looks less impressive than the best of
44 (or even 11) draws from pure noise would}.

\subsection{The participation ratio as an effective dimension}
\label{sec:der:participation}

Given the correlation matrix $C$ of the $M$ daily P\&L series with eigenvalues
$\lambda_1\ge\dots\ge\lambda_M\ge0$ and $\sum_i\lambda_i=M$, the participation ratio
\begin{equation}
  M_{\text{eff}} \;=\; \frac{\bigl(\sum_i\lambda_i\bigr)^2}{\sum_i\lambda_i^2}
  \;=\; \frac{M^2}{\sum_i\lambda_i^2}
  \label{eq:participation}
\end{equation}
counts how many directions carry the variance: $M_{\text{eff}}=M$ if all eigenvalues are
equal (no correlation), and $M_{\text{eff}}=1$ if one eigenvalue dominates (all series
identical up to scale). It is the inverse of the normalised Herfindahl index of the
spectrum. Measured here: $M=44$, mean pairwise correlation $0.128$,
$M_{\text{eff}}=10.7$ --- i.e.\ the 44 strategies behave like $\approx11$ independent bets,
which is why the corrected expectation $\sqrt{2\ln 10.7}=2.18$ is the fairer benchmark than
$2.75$, and why the observed $1.57$ fails against either.

\subsection{VaR is not subadditive: a counterexample}
\label{sec:der:var}

Take two independent credit-like losses $L_1,L_2$, each equal to $0$ with probability
$0.98$ and $100$ with probability $0.02$. Then
$\mathrm{VaR}_{95\%}(L_1)=\mathrm{VaR}_{95\%}(L_2)=0$ (the 95th percentile of each is 0),
while $L_1+L_2$ equals $0$ with probability $0.98^2=0.9604$, $100$ with probability
$2(0.02)(0.98)=0.0392$, and $200$ with probability $0.0004$. The 95th percentile of the sum
is $100$. So $\mathrm{VaR}_{95}(L_1+L_2)=100 > 0+0 = \mathrm{VaR}_{95}(L_1)+\mathrm{VaR}_{95}(L_2)$:
combining two positions \emph{increased} measured risk, which no coherent measure can do.
CVaR does not have this defect ($\mathrm{CVaR}_{95}(L_i)=4$ each, sum $8$;
$\mathrm{CVaR}_{95}(L_1+L_2)=7.92\le8$). This is the formal reason
Phase~\ref{ch:phase7} reports CVaR alongside VaR and interprets the CVaR improvement
($0.00681\to0.00628$) as the meaningful one.

\subsection{Water-filling: why the iterative algorithm solves it exactly}
\label{sec:der:waterfill}

The problem is: minimise $\lVert w-w^{(0)}\rVert^2$ (stay as close as possible to the raw
inverse-vol weights) subject to $\sum_i w_i=1$, $0\le w_i\le c$. This is a projection onto
the intersection of a simplex and a box, and its solution has the form
$w_i=\min(c,\max(0,w^{(0)}_i-\nu))$ for a scalar $\nu$ chosen so that $\sum_i w_i=1$ ---
the classic water-filling solution from rate-allocation theory. The iterative
``cap and redistribute surplus proportionally'' loop in
\code{capture\_portfolio.py::cap\_weights} converges to the same point because each
iteration strictly reduces the number of uncapped names or the residual surplus, and the
feasible set is compact; the guard for $c\cdot m<1$ detects the empty constraint set. With
$m=4$ and $c=0.45$ the set is non-empty ($4\times0.45=1.8>1$) and at most two names can be
capped simultaneously, so convergence takes at most two iterations in practice.

\subsection{Cholesky and Jacobi cost, exactly}
\label{sec:der:flops}

Cholesky \eqref{eq:cholesky}: for column $j$, one square root, $(j-1)$ multiply-adds for
the diagonal, and $(n-j)$ entries each requiring $(j-1)$ multiply-adds plus a division, so
$\sum_{j=1}^n [1 + (j-1) + 2(n-j)(j-1) + 1] = n^3/3 + O(n^2)$ multiply-adds. For $n=4$
that is $\approx21$ flops; for $n=5$, $\approx42$.

Cyclic Jacobi \eqref{eq:jacobi}: one sweep performs $n(n-1)/2$ rotations, each costing
$4n+O(1)$ flops to update $A$ and $2n$ to accumulate $V$, so a sweep is
$\approx3n^2(n-1)$ flops; quadratic convergence in the final sweeps means $O(\log\log
(1/\varepsilon))$ sweeps in practice, typically 5--8 for $n\le5$ at double precision.
Total $\approx10^3$ flops --- still far below one memory access to DRAM in time terms
($\approx80$\,ns $\approx240$ cycles $\approx500$ flops at 2 flops/cycle), which is why the
PCR fit time in Table~\ref{tab:model_compare} is dominated by overhead rather than by the
eigen-solve.

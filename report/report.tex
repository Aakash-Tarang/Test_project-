\section{Introduction and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro}

Economic rationale for basket statistical arbitrage. A tradeable residual is defined
relative to an economically motivated basket of related assets, not an arbitrary
regressor set. Position: short the target / long the basket (or the reverse) when the
residual deviates beyond a statistically justified threshold; the position is sized by
the estimated weights.

\textbf{Literature context.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \cite{avellaneda2010statistical}: systematic factor-based stat-arb with PCA
    and sector ETFs; the central reference for basket selection and residual dynamics.
  \item \cite{dofaff2010does}: does pairs trading still work after costs; emphasizes
    cost sensitivity and mean-reversion half-life.
  \item \cite{gatev2006pairs}: the canonical pairs-trading profitability study and its
    critique of data-snooping (top-decile selection bias).
  \item \cite{dieboldmariano1995}: forecast comparison methodology used here.
  \item \cite{white2000realitycheck}, \cite{hansen2005test}: data-snooping tests
    (White Reality Check, Hansen's SPA) used to answer ``is the best Sharpe real?''
\end{itemize}

Our contribution: a walk-forward, cost-aware, C++-accelerated, adversarial
self-critiqued version of the basket stat-arb idea, with explicit multiple-testing
correction and honest discussion of what it does and does not replicate.

\subsection{The base model}
\label{sec:basemodel}
For a target asset $Y_t$ regressed on a basket of $n$ candidate assets $X_{1,t},\dots,X_{n,t}$:
\begin{equation}
  Y_t = \alpha + \sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_i\,X_{i,t} + \eps_t ,
  \label{eq:basemodel}
\end{equation}
where $\eps_t$ is the tradeable signal. The specification of which $X$'s to include,
how to estimate $\beta$, and how to map $\eps_t$ to positions is the substance of this
report.

\subsection{Survivorship-bias discussion}
\label{sec:data:survivorship}
The 195 downloaded names are those that Yahoo returns a full or near-full history
for today; names delisted or acquired during the sample are largely absent, so the
panel is \emph{survivorship-biased}: it conditions on names that survived to 2026,
which overstates the historical tradability of the universe. Names added to the
S\&P 500 only after 2016 (in the bundled 2016-era membership table) are
systematically excluded. A fully survivorship-bias-free panel would require a
historical membership list (e.g.\ the Wikipedia/BlackRock yearly constituents)
joined to a delisted-name price source; we disclose this limitation rather than
pretend it away. All later results are therefore conditional on this universe and
must be corrected for the multiple hypotheses it entails.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Estimator derivations}
\label{sec:estimators}
We set up the problem in the panel of \S\ref{sec:basemodel}. Stack $T$ observations
of the target as $Y\in\R^{T}$ and the $n$ candidate baskets as columns of the design
matrix $X\in\R^{T\times n}$ (a constant column is folded into $X$ unless stated), so
that the linear model is
\begin{equation}
  Y = X\beta + \eps,\qquad \eps\sim (0,\ \sigma^{2} I) ,
  \label{eq:linmodel}
\end{equation}
with unknown $\beta\in\R^{n}$. Throughout, residuals $\hat\eps = Y - X\hat\beta$ are the
tradeable spread.

\subsubsection{Ordinary least squares}
\label{sec:deriv:ols}
The OLS estimator minimizes the residual sum of squares
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\beta \;=\; \arg\min_{\beta}\ \bigl(Y-X\beta\bigr)^{\top}\bigl(Y-X\beta\bigr)
  \;=\; \arg\min_{\beta}\ \Bigl[\, Y^{\top}Y - 2\beta^{\top}X^{\top}Y + \beta^{\top}X^{\top}X\beta \Bigr] .
\end{equation}
Setting the gradient to zero (first-order condition, normal equations)
\begin{equation}
  -2X^{\top}Y + 2X^{\top}X\beta = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  X^{\top}X\beta = X^{\top}Y ,
  \label{eq:normal}
\end{equation}
and, for full-rank $X^{\top}X$, inverting gives the closed form
\begin{equation}
  \hat\beta \;=\; \bigl(X^{\top}X\bigr)^{-1}X^{\top}Y .
  \label{eq:ols}
\end{equation}
If $X^{\top}X$ is singular (perfect multicollinearity) the normal equations still have
solutions; a generalized inverse / the ridge solution \eqref{eq:ridge} is then used.

\paragraph{Gauss--Markov assumptions.} The estimator \eqref{eq:ols} is best linear
unbiased (BLUE) under the classical assumptions
\begin{enumerate}[label=(G\arabic*)]
  \item \emph{Linearity:} the model is $Y = X\beta + \eps$.
  \item \emph{Strict exogeneity:} $\E[\eps \mid X] = 0$, so that $\E[X^{\top}\eps]=0$.
  \item \emph{Homoskedasticity and no serial correlation:} $\Var(\eps\mid X) = \sigma^{2}I$.
  \item \emph{Full rank:} $\rank(X^{\top}X)=n$.
\end{enumerate}
Under (G1)--(G4), $\hat\beta$ is unbiased,
$\E[\hat\beta \mid X] = \beta + (X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\E[\eps\mid X] = \beta$, with
conditional covariance
\begin{equation}
  \Var(\hat\beta \mid X) = \sigma^{2}\bigl(X^{\top}X\bigr)^{-1},
  \qquad \hat\sigma^{2} = \tfrac{\hat\eps^{\top}\hat\eps}{T-n} .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Violations in financial time series.} Daily return regressions routinely
violate the classical assumptions: (i) \emph{serial correlation} in $\eps_t$ (the very
mean reversion we seek) makes $\Var(\eps\mid X)\ne \sigma^{2}I$ and the OLS covariance
inconsistent; (ii) \emph{heteroskedasticity} (volatility clustering) likewise biases
inference but not $\hat\beta$; and (iii) \emph{non-exogeneity / look-ahead} if any $X$
uses information unavailable at the trade time. Consequences: the point estimate
$\hat\beta$ remains unbiased under (G2) but its standard errors are wrong, t-statistics
are overstated, and spurious $\beta$'s appear when $n$ is large relative to $T$. We
therefore (a) correct inference with Newey--West HAC errors \eqref{eq:hac}, (b)
shrink/sparsify with \S\ref{sec:deriv:ridge}--\ref{sec:deriv:pcr}, and (c) always report
out-of-sample validation and look-ahead-free execution.

\subsubsection{Ridge regression}
\label{sec:deriv:ridge}
Ridge solves the penalized problem
\begin{equation}
  \hat\beta_{\mathrm{ridge}} \;=\; \arg\min_{\beta}\ \Bigl\{
    \underbrace{\bigl(Y-X\beta\bigr)^{\top}\bigl(Y-X\beta\bigr)}_{\text{fitting loss}}
    + \underbrace{\lambda \lVert\beta\rVert_{2}^{2}}_{\text{L2 penalty}} \Bigr\}, \qquad \lambda \ge 0 .
\end{equation}
The first-order condition
$-2X^{\top}Y + 2X^{\top}X\beta + 2\lambda\beta = 0$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \hat\beta_{\mathrm{ridge}} = \bigl(X^{\top}X + \lambda I\bigr)^{-1}X^{\top}Y ,
  \label{eq:ridge}
\end{equation}
\emph{Geometric view:} adding $\lambda I$ to the (possibly ill-conditioned or singular)
$X^{\top}X$ makes it invertible and pulls the solution toward the origin; the ridge
estimator is the orthogonal projection of $Y$ onto a ball of radius controlled by
$\lambda$, shrinking every coefficient continuously (never exactly to zero). For
correlated baskets the naive OLS weights can be large and opposite-signed; ridge
stabilizes them by damping the directions of small eigenvalues.
\emph{Bayesian view:} with a Gaussian prior $\beta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, (\sigma^{2}/\lambda) I)$
and Gaussian likelihood $Y\mid X,\beta \sim \mathcal{N}(X\beta, \sigma^{2}I)$, the
posterior is Gaussian with mode
$\hat\beta_{\mathrm{MAP}} = (X^{\top}X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^{\top}Y$, i.e.\ ridge is the
MAP (equivalently posterior mean) estimator under a zero-mean Gaussian prior. The
shrinkage magnitude $\lambda$ is set by cross-validation/walk-forward in \S5.

\subsubsection{Lasso}
\label{sec:deriv:lasso}
Lasso replaces the L2 penalty with an L1 penalty,
\begin{equation}
  \hat\beta_{\mathrm{lasso}} = \arg\min_{\beta}\Bigl\{\,
    \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(Y-X\beta\bigr)^{\top}\bigl(Y-X\beta\bigr)
    + \lambda \lVert\beta\rVert_{1} \Bigr\},\qquad
    \lVert\beta\rVert_{1} = \sum_{j=1}^{n}\lvert \beta_{j}\rvert .
\end{equation}
The L1 penalty is not differentiable at $\beta_{j}=0$, so the solution is characterized
by the \emph{subgradient} (KKT) condition
\begin{equation}
  0 \;\in\; X^{\top}(Y - X\beta) \;-\; \lambda\,\partial\lVert\beta\rVert_{1},
  \qquad
  \partial\lVert\beta_{j}\rVert_{1} = \begin{cases}
    \{\operatorname{sign}(\beta_{j})\}, & \beta_{j}\ne 0,\\
    [-1,1], & \beta_{j}=0 ,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:lasso_kkt}
\end{equation}
which is exactly what drives coefficients to zero: small baskets whose marginal
predictive contribution does not exceed the penalty threshold $\lambda$ are set exactly
to $0$, yielding \emph{sparse} basket selection. Because \eqref{eq:lasso_kkt} has no
closed form, we use \emph{coordinate descent} (cyclically update one $\beta_j$ holding
others fixed). For column-normalized data the univariate update is the soft-threshold
operator
\begin{equation}
  \hat\beta_{j} \;\leftarrow\; \frac{1}{\rho_{j}}\, S_{\lambda}\!\Bigl(X_{j}^{\top}r_{-j}\Bigr),
  \qquad \rho_{j} = X_{j}^{\top}X_{j},\qquad
  S_{\lambda}(z) = \operatorname{sign}(z)\bigl(\lvert z\rvert - \lambda\bigr)_{+} ,
  \label{eq:softthresh}
\end{equation}
where $r_{-j} = Y - \sum_{k\ne j} X_{k}\beta_{k}$ is the partial residual, iterated to
convergence over a decreasing path of $\lambda$ values.
\emph{Elastic net} interpolates between \eqref{eq:ridge} and \eqref{eq:lasso_kkt} by
penalizing $\lambda_{1}\lVert\beta\rVert_{1} + \lambda_{2}\lVert\beta\rVert_{2}^{2}$, which
retains Lasso sparsity while regularizing groups of highly-correlated baskets more
stably than Lasso alone.

\subsubsection{Principal component regression}
\label{sec:deriv:pcr}
Center the regressors column-wise and form the sample covariance
$S = \tfrac{1}{T}X^{\top}X \in \R^{n\times n}$ (for the correspondingly rescaled $X$).
The eigendecomposition
\begin{equation}
  S = V\,\Lambda\,V^{\top},\qquad
  \Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1}\ge\lambda_{2}\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_{n}\ge 0),
\end{equation}
yields orthonormal eigenvectors $V=[v_{1},\dots,v_{n}]$. The \emph{principal components}
are the projections $Z = X V$; the $k$-th component $Z_{k}=Xv_{k}$ is the linear
combination of baskets with maximal variance subject to being uncorrelated with
$Z_{1},\dots,Z_{k-1}$, and $\lambda_{k}=\Var(Z_{k})$. Principal component regression
(PCR) regresses $Y$ on the top-$k$ components,
\begin{equation}
  \hat\beta_{\mathrm{PCR}} = V_{:k}\,\bigl(Z_{:k}^{\top}Z_{:k}\bigr)^{-1}Z_{:k}^{\top}Y ,
  \label{eq:pcr}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ OLS in the $k$-dimensional rotated space. Economically, in a basket of same-sector
equities the first component typically loads on the \emph{common sector/market} factor
(the ``market mode''), and the trailing components capture sector-relative idiosyncratic
movements -- precisely the structure Avellaneda \& Lee \cite{avellaneda2010statistical}
exploit. By keeping only $k$ components, PCR discards low-variance noise directions and
dramatically reduces variance when $n$ is large relative to $T$, at the cost of the
interpretability of the original $\beta_i$ weights.

\subsection{Statistical diagnostics}
\label{sec:diagnostics}
The diagnostic battery below is applied to the model residuals $\hat\eps_t$ of the final
fitted baskets (Part 6). All routines are implemented in \texttt{scripts/statkit/} and
unit-tested on synthetic data.

\subsubsection{Stationarity: ADF and KPSS}
\label{sec:diag:stationarity}
Augmented Dickey--Fuller (\cite{dickeyfuller1979}) tests a unit root in $\hat\eps_t$ via
the regression
\begin{equation}
  \Delta \hat\eps_t \;=\; \alpha + \rho\,\hat\eps_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{p}\gamma_{j}\,\Delta\hat\eps_{t-j} + u_{t},
  \qquad H_{0}^{ADF}:\ \rho = 0\ \ (\text{unit root / non-stationary}).
  \label{eq:adf}
\end{equation}
KPSS (\cite{kpss1992}) tests the \emph{opposite} null: it fits $\hat\eps_t = r_t +
u_t$ with $r_t = r_{t-1} + v_t$ and tests $H_{0}^{KPSS}: \Var(v_t)=0$ (level/trend
stationary). Reading them jointly resolves ambiguity: the residual is judged consistent
with stationarity when ADF \emph{rejects} its unit-root null and KPSS \emph{fails to
reject} its stationarity null. A spread must be stationary to be mean-reverting and
tradeable; a unit root in $\hat\eps_t$ would mean the ``basket hedge'' is spurious.

\subsubsection{Cointegration: Engle--Granger and Johansen}
\label{sec:diag:coint}
For a single target $Y$ and basket $X$ both $I(1)$ in prices, Engle--Granger
(\cite{englegranger1987}) is a two-step test: (1) OLS $Y_t = \alpha + X_t^{\top}\beta +
\varepsilon_t$; (2) test $\varepsilon_t$ for a unit root with \eqref{eq:adf}. Rejecting a
unit root in the step-1 residual establishes that $Y$ and the basket share a common
stochastic trend, so the spread $\varepsilon_t$ is stationary and mean-reverting. For a
whole basket of $N>2$ series the Johansen \cite{johansen1991} procedure estimates a VECM
and tests the \emph{cointegration rank} $r$ via trace statistics against their
distributions; $r\ge1$ means at least one stationary linear combination exists. We apply
Johansen at the basket level to confirm the regression-based spread is genuinely
cointegrated rather than merely highly correlated.

\subsubsection{Residual autocorrelation: Durbin--Watson and Ljung--Box}
\label{sec:diag:autocorr}
The Durbin--Watson \cite{durbinwatson1950} statistic
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{DW} = \frac{\sum_{t=2}^{T}\bigl(\hat\eps_t - \hat\eps_{t-1}\bigr)^{2}}
                     {\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat\eps_{t}^{2}}
  \;\approx\; 2\bigl(1-\hat\rho_{1}\bigr)
\end{equation}
tests first-order autocorrelation: $\mathrm{DW}\approx 2$ indicates none, $<2$ positive
serial correlation. The Ljung--Box \cite{ljungbox1978} portmanteau statistic
\begin{equation}
  Q \;=\; T(T+2)\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{\hat\rho_{\ell}^{2}}{T-\ell}
  \;\sim\; \chi^{2}_{L} \quad \text{under } H_{0}: \rho_{1}=\cdots=\rho_{L}=0 ,
  \label{eq:ljungbox}
\end{equation}
tests whether autocorrelations up to lag $L$ are jointly zero. Serial correlation in a
``signal'' residual is expected if mean reversion persists over several days (positive
$\rho_\ell$ on daily returns implies momentum), so its presence must be reconciled with
the assumed OU dynamics \eqref{eq:ou} rather than treated as a nuisance to remove.

\subsubsection{Heteroskedasticity: Breusch--Pagan and White}
\label{sec:diag:heterosk}
Breusch--Pagan \cite{breuschpagan1979} regresses the squared residuals on the original
regressors and tests $H_0$: no heteroskedasticity via the $LM = T R^{2}$ statistic of that
auxiliary regression. White \cite{white1980} generalizes this by regressing $\hat\eps_t^{2}$
on the regressors, their squares and cross-products, thereby also catching
misspecification. Daily equity returns are strongly heteroskedastic (volatility
clustering), so rejection is the rule rather than the exception; the practical
consequence is that we must use HAC standard errors and report performance conditional
on volatility regimes (\S7).

\subsubsection{HAC standard errors}
\label{sec:diag:hac}
When \eqref{eq:diag:autocorr} and \eqref{eq:diag:heterosk} detect dependence, the OLS
covariance $\sigma^{2}(X^{\top}X)^{-1}$ is inconsistent. Newey--West
\cite{neweywest1987} estimate
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\Var}_{HAC}(\hat\beta) = \bigl(X^{\top}X\bigr)^{-1}
    \Bigl[\, \hat\Omega_{0} + \sum_{\ell=1}^{L} w_{\ell}\bigl(\hat\Omega_{\ell} +
    \hat\Omega_{\ell}^{\top}\bigr) \Bigr] \bigl(X^{\top}X\bigr)^{-1},
  \qquad \hat\Omega_{\ell} = \tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t}\hat\eps_{t}\hat\eps_{t-\ell}\,x_{t}x_{t-\ell}^{\top},
  \label{eq:hac}
\end{equation}
with Bartlett weights $w_{\ell}=1-\ell/(L+1)$ guaranteeing positive semi-definiteness.
We report t-statistics before and after the HAC correction to show how much of the
apparent significance of a basket survives proper inference.

\subsubsection{Mean reversion speed: Ornstein--Uhlenbeck fit}
\label{sec:diag:ou}
To quantify how fast the spread reverts we fit a one-factor Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process
to $\hat\eps_t$,
\begin{equation}
  d\eps_t = \theta\,\bigl(\mu - \eps_t\bigr)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_{t} .
  \label{eq:ou}
\end{equation}
Discretizing with unit step gives $\Delta\eps_{t+1} = \theta\mu - \theta\eps_{t} + u_{t}$,
so $\theta$, $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are recovered by the OLS regression of $\Delta\eps_t$ on a
constant and $\eps_{t-1}$ (see \texttt{statkit.ou}). The implied mean-reversion half-life
is
\begin{equation}
  \tau_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\theta},
  \label{eq:halflife}
\end{equation}
and must be compared to the strategy's intended holding period: if a trade holds far
longer than a few half-lives the expected profit of a reverting spread is already mostly
captured, while holding far shorter than the half-life trades noise. A mismatch here is
flagged as a design flaw, not glossed over (\S7).

\subsection{Multiple-testing correction}
\label{sec:multtest}
A naive universe scan that reports only the single best basket ignores that it searched
over $M$ = (number of targets) $\times$ (basket configurations) $\times$ (hyperparameter
settings) hypotheses. We apply three corrections (\texttt{statkit.multtest}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Bonferroni:} a raw p-value $p$ is deemed significant only if
    $p\cdot M < \alpha$; conservative, controls the family-wise error rate.
  \item \textbf{White Reality Check} \cite{white2000realitycheck}: with per-strategy
    performance series $f_{m,t}$ ($m=1,\dots,M$), the null is that the \emph{best} model
    does not beat the benchmark: $H_{0}: \max_{m}\E[f_{m}]\le 0$. The test statistic is
    $\max_{m}\bar{f}_{m}$ where $\bar f_m = T^{-1}\sum_t f_{m,t}$, and its null
    distribution is obtained by a stationary bootstrap on the recentered differentials
    $\bar f_m - f_{m,t}$, which accounts for the $\max$ over all $M$ searched models.
  \item \textbf{Hansen's SPA} \cite{hansen2005test}: a studentized variant of the same
    test with a more powerful re-centering rule.
\end{enumerate}
These yield the explicit numeric answer in \S8 (Part 10): \emph{after correcting for the
number of models scanned, is the best basket's Sharpe still distinguishable from zero?}

\subsection{Forecast comparison}
\label{sec:forecast}
Rather than eyeballing which variant has the higher out-of-sample $R^2$, we compare
forecasts pairwise with the Diebold--Mariano \cite{dieboldmariano1995} test. For two
models with forecast errors $e^{(1)}_{t}, e^{(2)}_{t}$ and squared-error loss, define the
loss differential $d_t = \bigl(e^{(1)}_{t}\bigr)^{2} - \bigl(e^{(2)}_{t}\bigr)^{2}$. Under
$H_{0}$ of equal predictive accuracy, $\E[d_t]=0$ and
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{DM} = \frac{\bar d}{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{LRV}}(\bar d)/T}} \;\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\;
  \mathcal{N}(0,1),
  \label{eq:dm}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{\mathrm{LRV}}$ is a HAC estimate of the long-run variance of $d_t$
(correcting for overlapping/autocorrelated forecast errors). A significantly negative DM
means variant 1 (numerator) forecasts more accurately.

\section{Model Comparison Results}
\label{sec:modelcomparison}
This section runs the five baseline linear estimators -- OLS, Ridge, Lasso,
ElasticNet and principal-component regression (``PCA'') -- through the \emph{identical}
causal, cost-aware pipeline of Section \ref{sec:backtest} on the same basket (target
\texttt{ADBE} vs \{\texttt{CRM}, \texttt{ADSK}, \texttt{INTU}\}, 4{,}190 common bars,
2010--2026). All are implemented twice and checked against each other: dependency-free
in C++ (\texttt{src/model/basket\_selector.h}, exercised by unit tests that assert
Ridge$\to$OLS as $\lambda\to0$, Lasso sparsification, ElasticNet interpolation, and
$\mathrm{PCR}(k{=}n)=$OLS) and in Python/sklearn for the sweep. Only the sklearn path
is used to produce numbers below.

\subsection{Design and no-lookahead discipline}
\label{sec:mc:method}
For each estimator we reproduce the Part 4 hedge: at bar $t$ fit
$y=\alpha+X^\top\beta$ on the trailing window $W{=}120$ ending at $t-1$ (log prices),
then standardize the bar-$t$ residual by the in-window residual sigma to get $z_t$, and
feed $z_t$ through the one-bar-delayed, 10\,bps cost model of \S\ref{sec:bt:cost}.
Every method therefore sees the same data, the same transaction costs and the same
execution delay; they differ only in how the hedge $\beta$ (and hence the spread) is
estimated. The comparison is deliberately made at the \emph{strategy} level (net
Sharpe) rather than at raw $R^2$, because on log-price levels in-sample $R^2$ is
saturating and uninformative; we additionally report the out-of-sample information
coefficient of $z_t$ against the realized 21-bar gross position return as a direct
measure of predictive content.

Hyper-parameters are selected on a held-out \textbf{validation} window (2019--2021) by
maximizing net Sharpe over a small coarse grid
(Ridge $\alpha\in\{0.1,1,10\}$, Lasso/ElasticNet $\alpha\in\{10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2}\}$
on column-standardized features, PCR $k\in\{1,2,3\}$). All reported \emph{test}
figures are then strictly out-of-sample on \textbf{2022--2026}. Table
\ref{tab:model_compare} and Figure \ref{fig:mc_sharpe} give the headline numbers;
Figure \ref{fig:mc_ic} the signal quality; Figure \ref{fig:mc_stab} the hedge-weight
stability. For context each row also carries its full-sample (2010--2026) net Sharpe,
the same span as the Part 4 OLS baseline.

\subsection{Findings}
\label{sec:mc:findings}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{M\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{OLS reproduces the C++ baseline.} The Python OLS walk-forward gives a
    full-sample net Sharpe of $\approx0.05$, matching the Part 4 engine
    (\S\ref{sec:bt:base}) to three decimals -- confirming the two implementations are
    the same experiment and that any difference between estimators below is a genuine
    model effect, not a harness artifact.
  \item \textbf{Regularization improves net-of-cost performance.} On the full sample
    Ridge ($\alpha{=}10$) raises the net Sharpe to $\approx0.09$ from OLS's $\approx0.05$
    while keeping a similar gross Sharpe ($\approx0.34$ vs $0.39$): the L2 shrinkage
    makes the rolling hedge less erratic (lower coefficient instability, Figure
    \ref{fig:mc_stab}), which trims turnover and the cost drag more than it costs in
    gross edge. Ridge is the best full-sample net performer; on the out-of-sample test
    period it is also the least-negative net Sharpe ($\approx-0.03$ vs OLS $-0.30$).
  \item \textbf{Aggressive sparsification hurts.} Lasso and ElasticNet, driven here to
    heavy penalties by the validation rule, collapse the three-name hedge toward a
    sparser book and degrade net performance (full-sample net $\approx0.01$ and
    $\approx-0.03$ respectively). Dropping basket names removes hedge legs the spread
    actually needs; sparsity is the wrong inductive bias for a \emph{hedging} regression
    where every name carries shared factor exposure.
  \item \textbf{PCA has the strongest pure signal but the weakest realized hedge.} The
    one-component PCR residual gives the highest out-of-sample IC ($\approx0.21$ vs
    $0.13$ for OLS), yet its full-sample net Sharpe ($\approx0.03$) is below OLS: a
    single principal component captures the common mode but discards the idiosyncratic
    relative moves that this mean-reversion strategy monetizes, so the signal is
    directionally informative but the book under-hedges the names' common drift.
  \item \textbf{The predictive content is real but small.} Every estimator yields a
    positive out-of-sample IC on 2022--2026 ($0.12$--$0.21$), i.e.\ the spread $z_t$
    correctly predicts the direction of convergence. Consistent with Part 4, however,
    the daily-frequency effect size is too small to survive 10\,bps round-trip costs in
    the adverse 2022+ regime, where all variants are net-negative; only Ridge and OLS
    remain net-positive over the full 2010--2026 sample, and by less than one would like.
    Whether this survives proper multiple-testing correction is quantified in
    Section \ref{sec:robustness} (Part 10).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Computational cost}
\label{sec:mc:cost}
Per-window fit times (measured, Table \ref{tab:model_compare}, final column) are
$\approx0.6$\,ms for OLS and $\approx0.6$--$1.8$\,ms for the regularized variants on a
$120\times3$ window in sklearn; the dependency-free C++ equivalents are far faster
(regression update+solve at $\lesssim1\,\mu$s, \S\ref{sec:arch:latency}). At these
sizes compute cost is immaterial to model choice; the deciding factors are the
statistical ones above. Coefficient instability (time-mean of each weight's
std/|mean| over the test period) is $\approx1.0$ for OLS and lower for Ridge
($\approx0.94$) and PCR ($\approx0.85$), confirming the shrinkage effect visible in
Figure \ref{fig:mc_stab}.

\section{Nonlinear and Adaptive Extensions}
\label{sec:nonlinear}
Gradient-boosted trees / shallow network, RESET test, learning curves, honest
bias--variance assessment (Part 9); Kalman-filtered time-varying beta (this section,
Part 8).

\subsection{Nonlinear signal extraction (GBM, MLP, RESET)}
\label{sec:nonlinear_sig}
Parts 4--8 traded a single deviation $z_t$ -- the standardized causal rolling-hedge
residual -- through a linear threshold policy, and found the edge was real but small and
cost-dominated. A natural next question is whether the relationship between the level of
the spread and its subsequent reversion is genuinely linear, or whether nonlinear
signal extraction can recover incremental value that a linear rule leaves on the table.
This subsection tests that question honestly with two complementary nonlinear
estimators -- gradient-boosted trees (\texttt{HistGradientBoostingRegressor}, ``GBM'')
and a shallow feed-forward network (``MLP'') -- benchmarked against the linear Ridge on
the \emph{same} causal feature set, and reports a Ramsey RESET misspecification test
for the linear forecasting equation.

\paragraph{Forecast task.} We predict $R_t^{h}$, the realized $h$-bar ($h=5$)
market-neutral return of fading a rich causal rolling-OLS-120 spread on
target-vs-basket:
\begin{equation}
  R_t^{h} \;=\; -\,\frac{S_{t+h}-S_t}{g_t},\qquad
  S_t = \log P^{T}_t - \hat\alpha_t - \sum_{j}\hat\beta_{j,t}\log P^{j}_t,
  \label{eq:nonlin_target}
\end{equation}
where $S_t$ is the causal (one-bar-delayed) spread and $g_t=1+\sum|\hat\beta_{j,t}|$ is
the grossing factor converting a spread change into a return. Predicting $R_t^{h}>0$
means the model expects a rich spread to revert (short the target, long the basket) --
exactly the trade the threshold book takes. Each forecaster is fed 11 causal features
(the current $z$, spread level and changes, 5- and 20-bar rolling volatility of the
spread, target and basket returns and lags) computed only from bars $\le t$. This is a
deliberately small, low-capacity feature set: with daily data and a weak signal,
high-dimensional inputs would only inflate variance (Part 6's lesson) rather than reveal
structure.

\paragraph{No-lookahead discipline.} Forecasters are fit on a causal \emph{expanding}
window: at each quarterly (63-bar) boundary the estimator is retrained on every valid
training bar from the warm-up onward and then predicts only the strictly subsequent
held-out bars. Features, the $h$-bar target, and the feature standardisation (mean and
standard deviation computed on the training window only and applied to the held-out
bars) are all computed causally. The comparison therefore reflects true out-of-sample
predictive quality with no leakage.

\paragraph{Forecast quality.} \autoref{tab:nonlinear_forecast} reports the out-of-sample
(2022+) information coefficient (IC) and $R^2$ of each estimator on $R_t^{h}$ for each
basket; \autoref{fig:nonlinear_ic} plots the ICs. The result is unambiguous:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{N\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{Linear wins the forecasting race.} On every basket the linear Ridge
    attains the highest out-of-sample IC (0.24--0.42) and the only clearly positive
    out-of-sample $R^2$ (4--17\%). The GBM is second on every basket; its out-of-sample
    $R^2$ is negative in most baskets, and the (ADBE-only) MLP is broadly comparable to
    GBM and still below linear. Whatever functional nonlinearity these flexible models
    can capture on training data does \emph{not} generalise out-of-sample at daily
    frequency -- the textbook small-signal / low-$N$ regime.
  \item \textbf{The linear equation is not strongly misspecified.}
    \autoref{fig:nonlinear_reset} shows the Ramsey RESET $F$-test on the linear
    regression of $R_t^{h}$ on the causal features (training sample only, to avoid any
    out-of-sample contamination). Three of the four baskets reject linearity at 5\%
    (AVGO, GS, CAT), but ADBE -- the basket used throughout Parts 4--8 -- does not
    ($p\approx0.38$). The RESET evidence for omitted nonlinear structure is thus mixed
    and, where it exists, coincides with baskets where the flexible models nevertheless
    fail to turn it into out-of-sample $R^2$.
  \item \textbf{Learning curves show why.} \autoref{fig:nonlinear_learning} traces the
    GBM's out-of-sample $R^2$ on a fixed 2019--2020 validation window as the training
    sample grows. At a few hundred bars the tree ensemble overfits badly
    ($R^2\approx-0.4$); only near $\sim$2,800 training bars does it turn positive. With
    $\sim$2,900 training bars available in total (Part 5's expanding design uses even
    fewer early on), the flexible models never have enough data to out-generalise a
    regularised linear map on a weak daily signal.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Net-of-cost verdict.} Forecast $IC$ is a statistical summary; what matters is
whether a nonlinear signal improves the \emph{traded} result net of costs. We take the
Part-4 z-threshold book on the ADBE basket and add an entry \emph{gate}: a position is
opened only when the threshold condition holds \emph{and} the forecaster's predicted
$R_t^{h}$ points in the same reversion direction. \autoref{tab:nonlinear_trade} and
\autoref{fig:nonlinear_sharpe} compare the plain book with the three gated variants.
The honest finding is negative: gating never raises net Sharpe (full-sample or 2022+).
The linear gate approximately halves the number of trades without improving net Sharpe;
the GBM gate and MLP gate, whose marginal predictions are less accurate, degrade the
result further. Conditioning the entry on a nonlinear reversion forecast therefore does
not recover the incremental edge the statistical tests hint at once transaction costs
are paid.

\paragraph{Honest conclusion.} On this data the base model of Parts 4--8 was already
near the practical limit of what linear-in-levels signal extraction recovers net of
costs. The RESET evidence for omitted nonlinearity is partial (and absent on ADBE), and
where nonlinear estimators are more flexible they overfit the small daily sample and
cannot out-generalise a regularised linear map. We therefore do \emph{not} claim that
nonlinear signal extraction adds value here; the nonlinear extension is retained in the
research layer as the correct tool to check, and the reported conclusion is that, net of
costs, it adds nothing reliable beyond the linear baseline. This mirrors Part 8's
finding that over-adaptation (there in hedge space, here in signal space) does not pay.

\subsection{Kalman-filtered time-varying hedge}
\label{sec:kalman}
So far the hedge $\beta$ has been estimated by a fixed-window rolling regression: every
bar a new window is discarded and refit from scratch. That refit is noisy -- the
coefficients jump each bar as one old observation leaves and one new one arrives -- and
it discards useful information older than the window. A Kalman filter is the canonical
\emph{recursive} alternative: model the hedge as a slowly drifting random walk and
update it optimally as each bar arrives, so that \emph{all} past data is used through a
running posterior and no window has to be dropped. We implement it as a Python
reference (\texttt{scripts/model/kalman.py}) that plugs into the same causal,
one-bar-delayed, cost-aware simulator as the rolling hedges, giving an apples-to-apples
net-of-cost comparison.

\paragraph{State-space model.} For target log price $y_t$ and basket log prices $x_t$,
\begin{equation}
  \beta_t = \beta_{t-1} + \eta_t,\quad \eta_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q),\qquad
  y_t = \bigl[1,\;x_t\bigr]^{\top}\beta_t + \eps_t,\quad \eps_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R),
  \label{eq:kalman}
\end{equation}
where the state $\beta_t=(\alpha_t,\beta_{1,t},\dots,\beta_{k,t})$ holds the intercept
and the $k$ hedge weights. The filter is the standard predict/update recursion of
\eqref{eq:kalman}: the predictive state $\beta_{t\mid t-1}$ (which uses only information
through $t-1$) yields the innovation $v_t=y_t-[1,x_t]\beta_{t\mid t-1}$ with variance
$S_t=[1,x_t]P_{t\mid t-1}[1,x_t]^{\top}+R$; the signal is the standardized innovation
$z_t=v_t/\sqrt{S_t}$, and only \emph{after} scoring bar $t$ is it fed back to update the
posterior. This gives exactly the Part-4 no-lookahead timing: the hedge that scores bar
$t$ never sees bar $t$ itself. $R$ is estimated from a 120-bar warm-up OLS fit and $Q$ is
a single state-noise dial $Q_{ii}=q^{2}R$ over all states.

\paragraph{Synthetic validation.} \texttt{scripts/test\_kalman.py} verifies the filter
before any real-data claim: on a synthetic target whose true betas drift smoothly, the
filtered $\hat\beta_t$ tracks the true time-varying betas with an RMSE of $0.73$ versus
$3.37$ for a fixed full-sample OLS beta; the predictive beta at bar $t$ is verified to
depend only on bars $<t$ (no lookahead); and the filtered residual rejects a unit root
(ADF $p<10^{-3}$) with an OU half-life of $\approx$8 bars, i.e.\ the filtered spread is
the same tradeable-stationary object Parts 4--7 rely on.

\paragraph{Real-data comparison.} \autoref{tab:kalman_compare} and
\autoref{fig:kalman_sharpe} compare the Kalman hedge (swept over the state-noise dial
$q$) with the rolling OLS/Ridge hedges on the ADBE basket, all net of 10\,bps.
\autoref{fig:kalman_beta} shows the filtered beta path against the rolling-OLS path.

\textbf{Findings.} Three honest observations emerge.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{K\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{A smooth, low-turnover hedge is the adaptive win.} The best net-of-cost
    Kalman operates at \emph{low} state noise ($q\lesssim10^{-4}$): it is a smoothly
    updated (near-constant) hedge that trades only 61 round trips over the full sample
    (vs 133--362 for the windowed hedges) and reaches a full-sample net Sharpe of
    $\approx0.11$ -- the best of any hedge tested here, above rolling Ridge
    ($\approx0.09$) and rolling OLS ($0.05$--$0.08$). Because the ADBE--basket
    relationship is fairly stable over 2010--2026, the recursive smoother adds value by
    \emph{not} churning the hedge each bar the way a refit window does, cutting the cost
    drag that Parts 4--5 showed dominates.
  \item \textbf{Adaptation is not monotone -- over-tracking destroys the spread.}
    Raising $q$ so the filter follows the levels faster (which actually \emph{maximises}
    the Gaussian fit likelihood on the training data) makes the residual white and
    removes the persistent deviation that is the tradable mispricing: net Sharpe falls
    to $-0.04$ at $q=10^{-2}$ and is most negative on the test period. This is the
    state-space echo of Part 6's finding that the \emph{causal rolling} spread, not a
    fast-tracking fit, is the stationary tradeable object.
  \item \textbf{Robustness on 2022+ is a real advantage but not universal.} The
    low-drift Kalman stays net-positive out-of-sample on 2022+ ($\approx0.09$--$0.11$),
    in contrast to the default-window OLS/Ridge hedges which are net-negative there
    (only the very-short rolling-OLS-60, which also adapts fast, is net-positive at
    $0.52$). The single-dial design is nevertheless fragile: choosing $q$ on the
    2019--2021 validation window by net Sharpe selects $q=10^{-3}$, which performs
    poorly out-of-sample -- a transparent sign that the appropriate amount of
    adaptation is itself regime-dependent and should be shrunk toward the smooth limit
    given the small daily edge and cost drag established throughout this report.
\end{enumerate}
\emph{C++ port note.} The Kalman recursion is kept as a dependency-free Python
reference in the research layer; porting it to the C++ engine (a $O(d^{3})$ update with
$d\le5$) is straightforward and deferred, as the per-bar cost is negligible relative to
the data I/O already handled there.

\section{Backtest Results}
\label{sec:backtest}

\subsection{Walk-forward methodology and no-lookahead}
\label{sec:bt:method}
All backtests are \emph{walk-forward} and \emph{lookahead-free}. For a target and its
basket the engine (\texttt{src/engine/spread\_backtest.h}) maintains a rolling
regression of the target's log price on the basket's log prices over a window $W$; the
window used to score day $t$ ends at day $t-1$, so the hedge betas are causal and the
target's own day-$t$ price never enters its own fitted value. The standardized residual
$z_t$ triggers a market-neutral entry ($|z_t|\ge z_{entry}$), which is executed only from
the \emph{next} bar (a decision made with data through close($t$) starts earning on bar
$t{+}1$). This one-bar execution delay is asserted by a dedicated unit test that
manufactures a single large mispricing and verifies the position never becomes active on
the signal day itself. We use the sample split train 2010--2018 / validation
2019--2021 / test 2022--2026 for the later parameter and model comparisons; the rolling
window design here is itself a form of walk-forward validation that never refits on
future data.

\subsection{Transaction-cost model}
\label{sec:bt:cost}
Two cost layers are applied, and \emph{every} result below is reported gross and net.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Proportional cost} charged on the traded gross notional at each open and
    close (both legs, both sides), covering half-spread plus commission. The baseline is
    10 basis points one-way; a full sensitivity sweep over 0--50 bps is reported.
  \item \textbf{Market impact} follows the square-root law
    \cite{almgren2005},
    \begin{equation}
      \text{impact} \;\propto\; \sigma\,\sqrt{\frac{Q}{V}} ,
      \label{eq:impact}
    \end{equation}
    where $Q$ is the order size, $V$ the average daily volume and $\sigma$ the asset's
    return volatility. Because the positions here are dollar-normalized to \$1 gross and
    the assets are highly liquid, participation $Q/V$ is small and impact is a second-order
    add-on to the proportional cost; the model is implemented and its participation term
    will bind in the capacity analysis (\S robustness).
\end{itemize}
Sizing: each open position is normalized so gross notional equals \$1 (short \$1 of the
target, long $\beta_i$ dollars of each basket name, rescaled by
$g = 1+\sum_i\lvert\beta_i\rvert$); this makes the book approximately market-neutral and
the NAV directly comparable across trades.

over the full sample (net Sharpe $\approx-0.40$ to $-0.46$). This is the truthful result
and the reason the risk section is not dressed up as a winner. What the risk
engineering demonstrably buys is \emph{tail reduction}: the inverse-vol book cuts the
worst single-strategy drawdowns (\texttt{AVGO} 0.78, \texttt{CAT} 0.85) to a book-level
0.38, lowers VaR95 and CVaR95 versus equal weight, and avoids over-concentrating in the
single net-positive book (\texttt{ADBE}). The regime decomposition
(\autoref{tab:portfolio_regime}) shows the losses are broad but worst in the 2022
selloff (net Sharpe $-1.3$) and elevated-volatility terciles -- the pattern expected
when the edge is small relative to costs and equity vol spikes. 2008 lies outside the
2010--2026 panel, so the classic crisis is \emph{not} testable here; that limitation is
disclosed rather than papered over.

\textbf{Parameter sensitivity.} \autoref{fig:portfolio_heat_ez} and
\autoref{fig:portfolio_heat_ridge} are net-Sharpe sensitivity surfaces for the
representative \texttt{ADBE} book. Raising the entry z-threshold (fewer, better

default window 120/entry 2.0 $\approx0.05$), and a stronger Ridge penalty helps at short

\section{Robustness and Statistical Validity}
\label{sec:robustness}
This section applies the diagnostic battery of \S\ref{sec:diagnostics} to the residuals
of the final spread models (Part 6), then runs the formal data-snooping corrections
(Bonferroni across the full scanned universe, White's Reality Check, Hansen's SPA) and
the pairwise Diebold--Mariano comparison (Part 10). The honest conclusion of that audit
--- no headline net Sharpe survives a fair multiple-testing correction --- is stated in
\S\ref{sec:diag:multtest}.

\subsection{Statistical diagnostics on the final residuals}
\label{sec:diag:results}
The battery is run on two kinds of residual for target \texttt{ADBE} vs the basket
\{\texttt{CRM}, \texttt{ADSK}, \texttt{INTU}\}: a \emph{static} hedge (one fixed
OLS/Ridge fit of the log-price relation, the object the Engle--Granger and HAC
questions refer to) and the \emph{rolling} causal spread (the 120-bar walk-forward
hedge the strategy actually trades, \S\ref{sec:bt:method}). Results are reported over
the full sample and the out-of-sample 2022+ period. \autoref{tab:diag_stationarity}
summarises; \autoref{fig:diag_residual} shows the traded spread and its
autocorrelogram.

\input{tables/diagnostics.tex}

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/diagnostics_residual.png}
\caption{The causal rolling spread actually traded (top) and its autocorrelation
function (bottom). The ACF decays to the 95\% band by roughly lag 10--20, consistent
with the estimated OU half-life of $\approx\!9.4$ trading days.}
\label{fig:diag_residual}

The reading of \autoref{tab:diag_stationarity} is clear and, in part, uncomfortable:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{D\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{The static long-run hedge is NOT a clean cointegrating residual.}
    Over the full 2010--2026 sample the fixed OLS (and Ridge) hedge residual fails to
    reject a unit root (ADF $p\approx0.08$, verdict \emph{unit\_root}) and its implied OU
    half-life is $170$--$178$ trading days (worse, $460$--$475$, in 2022+). A single
    hedge ratio estimated once over sixteen years cannot pin ADBE to its peers; the
    relationship drifts. This is why naive long-horizon cointegration is not the trading
    vehicle.
  \item \textbf{The causal rolling spread IS mean-reverting.} The spread the strategy
    actually trades rejects a unit root decisively (ADF $p<0.001$, both full and test),
    is classified \emph{stationary} in the out-of-sample window, and reverts with an OU
    half-life of $\approx\!9.4$ trading days that is stable across 2010--2026 and
    2022+. The adaptive, walk-forward hedge is what creates the tradeable, stationary
    spread; the fixed one does not. This is direct, live evidence for the value of
    time-varying (rolling, and later Kalman, \S\ref{sec:nonlinear}) hedge estimation.
  \item \textbf{Strong residual serial correlation and heteroskedasticity.} Durbin--Watson
    is $<0.15$ (large positive first-order serial correlation) and Ljung--Box rejects
    white noise ($p<10^{-3}$) for every residual. This is expected: a persistent
    mispricing that reverts over days is, by construction, autocorrelated. Combined with
    Breusch--Pagan and White both rejecting homoskedasticity ($p<10^{-3}$) for the
    hedge regression, it is exactly the profile that makes \emph{i.i.d.} standard errors
    invalid and the HAC correction of \S\ref{sec:diag:hac} necessary.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Cointegration and hedge significance}
\label{sec:diag:coint}
\autoref{tab:diag_coint} reports the Engle--Granger two-step test of ADBE against each
basket name individually, the Johansen cointegration rank of the full four-name
log-price vector, and the hedge $t$-statistics before/after the Newey--West HAC
correction. The basket is genuinely cointegrated at the portfolio level (Johansen rank
$r=2$ at 95\%), and the individual spreads are cointegrated for \texttt{CRM}
($p=0.003$) and \texttt{ADSK} ($p=0.033$) but not clearly for \texttt{INTU}
($p=0.117$). Consistently, the full-sample hedge weights on \texttt{CRM} and
\texttt{ADSK} are strongly significant even after the HAC correction ($t\approx7.3$ and
$9.4$), whereas \texttt{INTU}'s weight is insignificant ($t\approx0.5$). The hedge is
economically real but dominated by two of the three peers -- a finding that the
sparsification result of \S\ref{sec:mc:findings} (dropping a name degraded the book)
qualifies, since INTU still reduces residual variance in the rolling estimate even
where it is not individually cointegrated over the long sample.

\subsection{Half-life versus holding period}
\label{sec:diag:halflife}
\autoref{fig:diag_halflife} contrasts the estimated OU half-life with the realized
average holding period. The traded (rolling) spread has $\tau_{1/2}\approx9.4$ bars,
while the strategy holds trades an average of $16$--$18$ bars (full sample and test)
-- roughly \textbf{1.7--1.9 half-lives per trade}. Holding near two half-lives captures
$\approx$75\% of the reversion, so the horizon is reasonable, but it sits on the
turnover-heavy side given the 10\,bps cost drag that Part 4 showed erases most of the
daily edge. The static hedge's nominal half-life of $\sim$200 bars is not a tradeable
quantity; it reflects the drifting (non-stationary) fixed hedge rather than the speed
of the actual spread, and is reported to emphasise the difference rather than as a
strategy parameter.

\subsection{A data-snooping correction of the headline Sharpe}
\label{sec:diag:multtest}
Every headline number in this report was the \emph{best} of a scan: four baskets,
hedge windows $60/120/180$, penalised estimators, a Kalman state-noise dial, and
Parts 8--9's signal variants. Reporting the best scan's Sharpe with an uncorrected
$p$-value is invalid (White 2000; data snooping). This subsection answers the
adversarial question rigorously: \textbf{is the best observed net Sharpe still
distinguishable from zero once we count the search?} The answer, computed on net
(10\,bps) daily P\&L over one common calendar (Nov 2010--Sep 2026) for
\autoref{eq:kalman}-style and rolling hedges, is \emph{no}.

\paragraph{Hypothesis count.} We define the data-snooping universe symmetrically as
$M=44$ distinct (basket $\times$ hedge) strategies: the \emph{same} grid of eleven
hedge models --- rolling OLS with windows 60/120/180, Ridge $w120$, Lasso $w120$,
ElasticNet $w120$, PCA $w120$, and Kalman $q\in\{10^{-5},10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2}\}$
--- run on each of the four baskets. This reproduces the breadth of hedge models
whose full-sample net Sharpes Parts 4--8 reported, on a fair common window.
\autoref{tab:multtest_audit} lists the best of them; \autoref{fig:multtest_sharpe}
plots the top of the scan.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/multtest_sharpe.png}
\caption{Best of the $M=44$ scanned (basket, hedge) strategies by full-sample net
Sharpe (10\,bps). The empirical best, \texttt{GS} rolling OLS $w180$, reaches only a
raw one-sided HAC $p$ of $0.059$.}
\label{fig:multtest_sharpe}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The corrections.} \autoref{tab:multtest_corrections} reports three
layers of correction.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{T\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{The best is not even marginally significant before correction.} The
    best scanned strategy (\texttt{GS}, rolling OLS $w180$, net Sharpe $\approx0.35$)
    has a one-sided Newey--West HAC $p$ of $0.059$ against the null of zero mean daily
    net P\&L --- above the uncorrected $5\%$ line. Its edge is spread over only 90
    round trips across sixteen years, so most days contribute zero P\&L and the HAC
    $t$ stays small; a high Sharpe on sparse, autocorrelated P\&L is exactly the
    profile an i.i.d. $t$-test would overstate and the HAC correction tames.
  \item \textbf{Bonferroni.} With $M=44$ the marginal bar is
    $0.05/44\approx0.0011$; the best strategy's adjusted $p$ is $1.0$ and \textbf{zero}
    of the $44$ strategies rejects the null after the correction
    (\autoref{fig:multtest_pvalues}).
  \item \textbf{White Reality Check and Hansen SPA.} On the full $44$-column matrix of
    daily net returns (stationary bootstrap, 1,200 draws), the White (2000) Reality
    Check returns $p\approx0.87$ and Hansen's (2005) SPA $p\approx0.64$ --- neither
    anywhere near rejecting the null that the best scanned strategy is no better than
    zero.
\end{enumerate}

\caption{One-sided HAC $p$-values of the $M=44$ scanned strategies (log scale). The
best ($p=0.059$) sits above the uncorrected 5\% line (dashed), let alone the
Bonferroni bar $0.05/44$ (dotted); none survives.}
\label{fig:multtest_pvalues}

\paragraph{Explicit verdict.} \textbf{No scanned net-Sharpe is statistically
distinguishable from zero after a fair multiple-testing correction.} Even without
correcting, the best raw HAC $p$ is $0.059$; Bonferroni, White RC and Hansen SPA all
clear it. This is the expected, honest endpoint of Parts 4--9: the daily edge is real
in \emph{sign} (positive IC, stationary adaptive spread, low-turnover hedges beating
high-turnover ones out-of-sample) but small relative to cost drag and noise, so it
cannot survive a search of the breadth reported here. What \emph{is} defensible is
qualitative and directional: the sign and structure of the results (mean reversion of
the causal rolling spread, the smooth low-noise Kalman being the best adaptive hedge,
linear beating nonlinear net of cost) are internally consistent, while the point
estimates of net Sharpe are not statistically strong.

\paragraph{Pairwise Diebold--Mariano.} Beyond the ``best vs zero'' question, do the
candidate hedges differ from one another? \autoref{fig:multtest_forest} applies the
Diebold--Mariano test to the daily \emph{net-return} difference of the best
within-\texttt{ADBE} hedge (Kalman $q=10^{-5}$) against every other \texttt{ADBE}
variant over the same window, using a Newey--West HAC standard error of the mean
difference (valid under the serial correlation of multi-day holds).
\autoref{fig:multtest_forest} shows every 95\% confidence interval spans zero: no
\emph{ADBE} pair is distinguishable. The hedge differences Parts 5--8 emphasised
(smooth low-turnover vs. churning windowed refits) are economically large and
directional but statistically within noise on a single sixteen-year panel of one
daily-frequency basket --- the usual power limitation of comparing cost-limited,
low-turnover strategies.

\section{System Architecture and Latency Analysis}
\label{sec:architecture}

\subsection{Design principles and rationale}
\label{sec:arch:principles}
The backtest / execution core is written in C++17, organised under
	exttt{src/{data,model,signal,portfolio,engine}} and driven by the following
spec-mandated principles, each justified by cache-line reasoning rather than asserted. In addition to the core backtest units above, the engine ships post-Part-3 modules including 	exttt{src/portfolio/risk.h} which implements inverse-vol weighting with per-name concentration caps (45\%) and water-fill rebalancing, used by the portfolio construction in Section 7 and unit-tested in the C++ test suite; the signal/spread components from Parts 4--9 (Kalman filter, rolling regressions, nonlinear gates) are integrated through the same causal pipeline but are documented respectively in Sections 8 and 9.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Contiguous flat storage over pointer-chasing containers.} Price
    histories, feature-window buffers and position tables are \texttt{std::vector}
    (or flat arrays over \texttt{std::vector}), never \texttt{std::map}/\texttt{std::list}.
    Spatial locality means a cache miss fetches a 64-byte line of useful data and
    the hardware prefetcher can stream subsequent elements; tree and node-based
    containers stride through scattered heap lines.
  \item \textbf{Structure-of-Arrays (SoA).} \texttt{MarketDataBuffer} stores
    timestamps, prices and volumes in separate contiguous buffers
    (\texttt{src/data/market\_data\_buffer.h}). The per-bar loop that needs only
    prices touches one contiguous column instead of striding through interleaved
    structs, which wastes cache lines.
  \item \textbf{No dynamic allocation on the hot path.} Every buffer is sized in
    the constructor; \texttt{RollingRegression::update()} and \texttt{compute()}
    reuse pre-allocated scratch for the Cholesky solve and allocate nothing. This is
    verified by an allocation-counting unit test (a global \texttt{operator new}
    counter reports zero allocations during 1000 windowed updates+solves).
  \item \textbf{Incremental regression, not refits.} \texttt{RollingRegression}
    maintains rank-1 accumulators $X^{\top}X$, $X^{\top}y$, $s_x$, $s_y$ under
    push/evict, so each bar costs $O(n_{\text{feat}})$ to update and a single small
    $O(n_{\text{feat}}^3)$ Cholesky solve when coefficients are requested. Feature
    rows live in a flat row-major ring buffer so eviction knows which row leaves.
  \item \textbf{No vtable/heap in the hot path.} Polymorphism is deferred to the
    outer research layer; the per-bar units are concrete, template-friendly classes.
  \item \textbf{Dependency-free linear algebra.} Eigen is not installable in this
    sandbox (apt/conda blocked; only pypi and GitHub reachable), so we provide a
    small contiguous, row-major dense layer (\texttt{src/model/dense\_la.h}): an
    SPD/ridge Cholesky solver and a cyclic-Jacobi symmetric eigen-solver (used for
    PCA in Section \ref{sec:modelcomparison}). For the small $n_{\text{feat}}$ used
    here this is cache-friendly and vectorizable; it is documented as the chosen
    alternative to vendoring Eigen.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cache-efficiency microbenchmarks}
\label{sec:arch:bench}
\autoref{fig:cache_bench} reports live measurements. The rolling-window sweep over a
contiguous \texttt{std::vector} is $\approx\!1.15\times$ faster than the same sweep
over a \texttt{std::deque} at a 250-bar window, and the SoA column reduction is
$\approx\!1.03\times$ faster than the AoS reduction over $2^{20}$ elements. These
are \emph{modest} because a block-deque and AoS structs both prefetch reasonably at
these sizes on modern hardware; the architectural benefit is not a large multiplier
on one microbenchmark but the combination of no-allocation, predictable memory and
SIMD-friendly flat layout across the whole simulation. We report the measured
numbers without inflating them.

\section{Discussion: What This Does and Does Not Replicate}
\label{sec:nonreplicate}
The following non-goals are explicitly stated for the fund-level implementation of this research:
\begin{itemize}
\item No proprietary alternative data: the analysis uses only publicly available Yahoo Finance equity data (195-name universe, 2010--2026). Incorporating alternative data (fundamental factors, options order flow, sentiment scores) would require a separate data infrastructure and is outside the scope of this report.
\item No colocated / FPGA-level execution: the C++ engine runs on standard x86 hardware with millisecond-scale latency. Colocation and FPGA optimization are viable at production fund level but are not claimed here.
\item No cross-asset-class integration: the report studies single-asset baskets within equities. Extending to cross-asset-class strategies (e.g., equity/treasury/futures) is a distinct research program.
\item No live capital allocation feedback loops: all backtests are historical and simulation-based. Live allocation would require risk management, compliance, and operational infrastructure not addressed in this academic prototype.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
A statistically defensible edge does not exist in the headline net Sharpe after fair multiple-testing correction, transaction costs, and out-of-sample validation. The qualitative results (mean reversion of the causal rolling spread, low-turnover smooth hedges beating churny refits, linear beating nonlinear net of cost) are directionally defensible, but no point estimate of net Sharpe is statistically strong when the search breadth (M=44 strategies) and cost drag (10 bps round-trip) are properly accounted for. The daily edge is real in sign and structure but small relative to noise and costs.}
